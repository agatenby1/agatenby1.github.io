Triage

Why do GPs triage at all?

Demand exceeds capacity, every single day. A typical practice list generates more requests for contact than there are appointments. If every patient simply self-booked, the book would fill with whoever rang fastest at 8am — and the routine slots would be gone before the person having their stroke got through. Triage exists so that clinical need, not queueing speed, decides who is seen first.

It happens in more ways than students expect. Telephone-first models, online forms (eConsult, AccuRx), video consulting (Near Me in Scotland), pharmacy pathways (Pharmacy First Scotland), and the receptionist's care-navigation script are all triage. Different practices weight these very differently, and all of them are defensible.

The evidence is messier than the enthusiasm. The ESTEEM trial (Lancet, 2014) found telephone triage redistributes workload rather than reducing it. An evaluation of "telephone-first" practices (BMJ, 2017) found large practice-to-practice variation and no overall saving. Triage is done because it is safer and fairer, not because it is cheaper.

And it is a clinical risk decision. Every call you park until tomorrow is a judgement made on incomplete information, usually without seeing the patient. That is why it is one of the harder things a GP does — and why it belongs in your training now. The GMC's Good Medical Practice (2024) expects you to recognise and work within the limits of that judgement.

The exercise

Level 1: how urgent is each call? Level 2: one action each — you cannot do everything. Level 3: the messy ones, where experienced GPs disagree. After you commit, Reveal shows a reasoned answer with references.

All cases are fictional and written for teaching. References orient you to the evidence; they are not a substitute for local pathways or your own senior review. Answers at Level 3 are defensible positions, not the only correct ones.
